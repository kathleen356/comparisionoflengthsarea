6.2 Solving problems involving the comparison of lengths and areas

We are learning to solve problems involving the comparison of lengths and areas, using appropriate units.

Success Criteria
    Teacher Note: This Grade 6 lesson builds on the foundational understanding from Grade 5. Students should now be able to apply area and perimeter concepts to solve authentic problems involving composite shapes, real-world contexts, and decision-making scenarios.
    Foundation

    Quick Recap: Perimeter and Area

    Before solving problems, let's refresh our understanding of how perimeter and area work together.

    Perimeter

    The distance around the outside of a shape.

    Measured in units like mm, cm, m or km. Used when we need to know how long a boundary is, such as fencing, framing, or trimming.

    Area

    The space covered inside a shape.

    Measured in square units like mm², cm², m² or km². Used when we need to know how much surface is covered, such as painting, flooring, or material.

    Key Formulas for Rectangles

    PERIMETER
    Perimeter = 2 × (length + width)
    AREA
    Area = length × width
    Teacher Note: Emphasise that perimeter is 1-dimensional (length) and area is 2-dimensional (square units). This conceptual difference is crucial for Grade 6 students to solve problems correctly.
    Guided Practice

    Worked Examples

    Work through these examples with your class. Think aloud about the steps, units, and strategies used.

    Worked Example 1: Calculating area and perimeter of rectangles

    Problem: Work out the area and perimeter of each rectangle.

    Step 1: Rectangle 1 (Blue), Perimeter

    Given: Length = 26 mm, Width = 10 mm

    P = 2(L + W)

    P = 2(26 + 10)

    P = 2(36) = 72 mm

    Step 2: Rectangle 1 (Blue), Area

    Given: Length = 26 mm, Width = 10 mm

    A = L × W

    A = 26 × 10 = 260 mm²

    Step 3: Rectangle 2 (Pink), Perimeter

    Given: Length = 13 cm, Width = 2 cm

    P = 2(L + W)

    P = 2(13 + 2)

    P = 2(15) = 30 cm

    Step 4: Rectangle 2 (Pink), Area

    Given: Length = 13 cm, Width = 2 cm

    A = L × W

    A = 13 × 2 = 26 cm²

    Reflection: the units matter. At first glance 260 mm² looks like a much bigger number than 26 cm², but we cannot compare them until they are in the same unit. There are 100 mm² in 1 cm², so 260 mm² = 2.6 cm². Rectangle 2 actually covers ten times as much space as Rectangle 1. Always convert to a common unit before you compare.

    Worked Example 2: Composite shapes, the phone case problem

    Problem: Olivia made a phone case prototype for her future mobile phone. She cut a square out for the camera. If she is using recycled cardboard boxes, how much material does she need?

    Step 1: Calculate outer rectangle area

    The phone case outer dimensions: 9 cm × 7 cm

    Aouter = 9 × 7 = 63 cm²

    Step 2: Calculate camera cutout area

    The square camera hole: 5 cm × 5 cm

    Acutout = 5 × 5 = 25 cm²

    Step 3: Calculate material needed

    Material used = Outer area − Cutout area

    Amaterial = 63 − 25 = 38 cm²

    Answer: Olivia needs 38 cm² of recycled cardboard to make her phone case prototype.

    Why this matters: In real life, we often need to calculate areas of composite shapes (shapes made from simpler shapes). Whether it's cutting a hole in a plate, designing a picture frame, or manufacturing products, we subtract areas to find what we actually need.
    Teacher Note: Have students draw their own diagrams for Example 2 and label all dimensions. Discuss real-world contexts: manufacturing, construction, design. Ask: "What other composite shape problems might you encounter in real life?"
